Value engineering began at General Electric in the 1940s, when shortages forced Lawrence Miles and colleagues to find substitutes for scarce parts and they noticed the substitutes were often cheaper and better. The method defines value as function divided by cost and insists that basic functions be preserved while cost falls. Done honestly, it asks what a scene is for and finds a cheaper way to do the same job.

In theme parks the phrase has a bad reputation, because it often arrives late and means cuts. Critics of the wider practice call it a euphemism for reducing cost, and the industry has its own folklore of missing scenes and second phases that never came. An IAAPA Funworld piece argues that last-minute value engineering is a symptom of an unrealistic first budget, and that honest early numbers prevent most of it. Good scope management names what must never change, usually the [story-bearing elements](../story-experience/theme-vs-decoration.md) and the finale, and flexes elsewhere. It is where [capital budgets](../operations-business/capital-budgets.md) meet [attendance models](../operations-business/roi-and-attendance-modeling.md), where the [screens versus sets debate](../show-technology/screens-vs-sets-debate.md) gets decided by price, and where the cost of a [blue sky](../design-process/blue-sky.md) promise finally comes due.
