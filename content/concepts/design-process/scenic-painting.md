Scenic painting comes straight from the theatre. Painters enlarge a designer's small maquette onto full-size surfaces and use scumbling, spattering, dry brushing and rag rolling to make flat material read as stone, timber or plaster from a distance and under stage light. Theme parks add two twists: guests get close enough to touch, and the paint must survive sun and rain for years.

Aging is the signature move. Joe Rohde describes it with an actor's analogy: a set, like a character, needs a history before the audience arrives. Chemical patinas, rust washes and dirt glazes in the corners tell guests that this street has been here for generations, which is what makes [environmental storytelling](../story-experience/environmental-storytelling.md) believable. Rockwork painters work the same way, laying a base colour, then multiple hues by spray and by hand, then an aging finish. The craft sits inside the wider family of [faux finishes](../sensory-environment/faux-finishes-and-aging.md), works hand in glove with [set dressing](../story-experience/set-dressing-and-props.md), and only looks right when checked under the final [lighting](../sensory-environment/lighting-mood.md), never under the shop's fluorescents.
