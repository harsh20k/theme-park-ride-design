A model is the cheapest way to make a mistake in three dimensions. Architects build quick study models from foam board and cardboard to test volumes and viewpoints, then presentation models to sell a design. Theme park teams add a third kind: the maquette, a sculptor's small clay study of a rock, a tree or a figure that will later be enlarged many times over.

Models answer questions screens struggle with. Walk around one at eye height and [forced perspective](../story-experience/forced-perspective.md) tricks either work or they do not. Lower your eye to a guest's scale and you see which [sightlines](../story-experience/sightlines.md) leak a backstage roof. Rockwork fabricators still build quarter-inch or half-inch scale models of wood, cardboard and clay to approve massing before welding a single armature. Imagineering in a Box ends its ride lesson with a physical model because moving pieces by hand teaches layout faster than any tutorial. Models are now often scanned and rebuilt digitally for [previs](../design-process/previs.md), and large ones are milled from CAD rather than carved, but the habit of holding the idea in your hands has not gone away.
