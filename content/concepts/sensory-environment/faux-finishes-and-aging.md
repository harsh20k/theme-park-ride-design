A faux finish is paint pretending to be a material; aging is paint pretending to be time. Both are scenic art scaled up to buildings that stand in sun and rain for decades. The sequence is layered: a base colour, a graining or mottle that imitates the real material, translucent glazes for depth, then a dark wash that pools in corners and recesses the way dirt does, and finally light dry-brushing on edges where hands and weather would have worn the surface. A Merlin scenic artist describes a raw umber wash over almost everything in the London Dungeon because nothing there may look clean.

Aging is storytelling, not decoration. Rust streaks below a bolt say years of water; a worn patch on a door says a thousand hands. That is why the finish follows the [story bible](../story-experience/story-bible.md): how old is this place, how rich were its owners, is it loved or abandoned? The same questions drive [set dressing and props](../story-experience/set-dressing-and-props.md), and the result is [environmental storytelling](../story-experience/environmental-storytelling.md) you read without noticing. It is [scenic painting](../design-process/scenic-painting.md) with one extra problem: real aging keeps happening. A finish must survive pressure washing and sun, so the [preventive maintenance](../operations-business/preventive-maintenance.md) plan carries the paint recipe.
