Daniel Stokols made the key distinction in 1972: density is a physical measure, people per unit of area, while crowding is a psychological state, the feeling that there are too many of them. The same density feels fine on a beach and intolerable in a lift. Edward T. Hall's proxemics adds the distances at which strangers feel too close, and shows they shift with culture and setting.

For designers, enclosure, not headcount, is the main dial. Low ceilings, blank walls and no view ahead make a modest crowd feel oppressive; height, openings, sky and a long sightline let a dense one feel like a festival. A themed queue uses both deliberately. Tight, dim [switchbacks](../human-factors/switchback-design.md) build pressure that the ride is about to relieve, while a plaza at a [pinch point](../masterplanning/pinch-points.md) gets a tall canopy and a view so that the real density there does not read as a crush. Guests report the felt measure, so [level of service](../masterplanning/level-of-service.md) grades should be checked against enclosure, not only counts. The topic sits beside [personal space and density](../human-factors/personal-space-and-density.md) and matters for [crowd behaviour](../human-factors/crowd-behavior.md): people who feel crowded push, stop or leave, and a little visible sky can prevent all three.
