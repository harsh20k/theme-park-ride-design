Rachel and Stephen Kaplan's 1989 book on environmental preference found that people like scenes that are both rich and legible. They framed it with four qualities: coherence, how easily a scene organises into a whole; complexity, how much there is to look at; legibility, how easy it would be to find your way; and mystery, the promise of more if you walk on. Complexity alone does not predict liking; complexity with coherence does.

Theme parks live at the high-complexity end; coherence keeps them from becoming noise. Coherence comes from a controlled palette, a consistent period and scale, and a hierarchy of what is big, bright and central against what is small, dull and peripheral. John Hench treated this ordering as the visual half of the show: every element agrees about where the eye goes. That is the daily business of [art direction](../design-process/art-direction.md), and the discipline behind [set dressing and props](../story-experience/set-dressing-and-props.md), where a hundred objects must still read as one shopkeeper's life. It is also the line between [theme and decoration](../story-experience/theme-vs-decoration.md): decoration adds complexity, theme supplies coherence. Coherence is tested by checking [sightlines](../story-experience/sightlines.md) for stray elements and asking where [visual attention](../human-factors/visual-attention.md) lands first; saturated accents work only against a quieter field.
