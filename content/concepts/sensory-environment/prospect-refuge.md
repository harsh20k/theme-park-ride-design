Jay Appleton's 1975 book The Experience of Landscape proposed that we judge places by an old survival instinct: can I see what is coming, and can I hide if I need to? He called these prospect and refuge, and argued that scenes offering both, a sheltered spot with a wide view, feel good even when nothing threatens us. A 2016 meta-analysis by Dosen and Ostwald found broad but uneven support across decades of preference studies, so treat it as a strong heuristic rather than a law.

Parks confirm it daily. The first benches taken are the ones with a wall or planting behind and a plaza, parade route or lagoon in front; the empty ones sit mid-plaza with people passing on all sides. That is why [rest areas](../masterplanning/rest-areas.md) work best as edges and porches, and why [food and retail placement](../masterplanning/food-and-retail-placement.md) favours a terrace looking onto activity. Designers also use the instinct in reverse. A queue that denies prospect, with high walls and tight [switchbacks](../human-factors/switchback-design.md), builds unease on purpose; a sudden overlook releases it, which is the mechanism behind [reveal and concealment](../story-experience/reveal-and-concealment.md). Managing [sightlines](../story-experience/sightlines.md) is thus also about rationing the view so that prospect, when granted, feels like a reward.
