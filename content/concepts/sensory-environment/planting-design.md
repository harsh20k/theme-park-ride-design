In a theme park a tree is a prop. Bill Evans, hired after landscaping Walt Disney's own garden, had about a year to turn 80 acres of orange groves into Disneyland, and his two-acre Jungle Cruise forest set the rule: choose plants for the picture, not the catalogue. He planted orange trees upside down because their roots read as exotic jungle branches. Fifty years on, Pandora mixed real Earth plants with sculpted alien species the same way.

Planting does four jobs. It places you, with palms for a lagoon and pines for a frontier. It screens, filling the sightline between guests and whatever stands beyond [the berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md), which is why planting is the cheapest cure for [visual intrusion](../human-factors/visual-intrusion.md). It sizes things: small-leaved, low plants near the eye and big ones far away stretch the apparent depth, a living form of [forced perspective](../story-experience/forced-perspective.md). And it keeps time, since a planting plan is a calendar of what flowers when. All of it sits inside [landscape architecture](../masterplanning/landscape-architecture.md), with one extra constraint: plants grow, so the planting plan carries a pruning plan. Disneyland's 1955 opening remains the reference case for landscape as show.
