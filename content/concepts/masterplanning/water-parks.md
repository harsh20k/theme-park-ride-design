A water park is a park whose attractions are pools, slides, splash areas, wave pools and lazy rivers. The form grew out of public swimming pools, and the United States now has over a thousand, with the World Waterpark Association and IAAPA as trade bodies. Disney's Typhoon Lagoon, opened in June 1989, is a model masterplan: a giant surf pool at the centre, a themed mountain behind it that hides the slide pipework and launch decks, a lazy river circling the whole site, and shaded beaches filling the gaps.

Planning differs from a dry park in a few ways. Capacity is counted as bather load, people in the water and on the decks, and the queue for a slide is a stairway, so tower height sets wait time. Guests are barefoot and wet, which drives surfaces, shade and [family facilities](../human-factors/family-facilities.md) such as lockers at the entry. The season is short outdoors, so [seasonality](../operations-business/seasonality-and-events.md) is extreme and indoor water parks exist for the same reason indoor theme parks do.

Ride design overlaps with the engineering land: [water coasters](../ride-systems/water-coaster.md) blur the boundary, and slides fall under the same [ASTM F2291](../safety-standards/astm-f2291.md) family of design standards. [Shade](../sensory-environment/shade-and-microclimate.md) is the single most important comfort investment.
