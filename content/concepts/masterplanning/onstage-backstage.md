Onstage and backstage is the theatre metaphor that organises a whole park. Anything a guest can see, hear or smell is onstage and must belong to the story. Anything else, from loading docks to break rooms to the chiller plant, is backstage and must be hidden. Disney took the language literally: employees are cast members, uniforms are costumes, and walking through a land out of character is a show problem, not just a staffing one.

The idea reportedly crystallised at Disneyland, where Walt is said to have seen a Frontierland cowboy cutting through Tomorrowland. The story may be polished, but the fix was real: Walt Disney World was planned so cast could reach any land from below through the utilidors, and later parks wrapped backstage behind facades, berms and planting.

In practice the separation is drawn on the masterplan as a line. Behind it go service roads, warehouses, wardrobe and the [central plant](../masterplanning/utilities-and-central-plant.md); in front, every object is set dressing. Where the two must touch, designers use [go-away green](../sensory-environment/go-away-green.md) paint, screening walls and [stage craft](../story-experience/theatre-staging.md) to make doors disappear. [Cast role play](../story-experience/cast-role-play.md) completes the illusion, protecting [suspension of disbelief](../human-factors/suspension-of-disbelief.md).
