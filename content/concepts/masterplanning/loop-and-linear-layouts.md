A loop layout threads the lands around a ring path, often circling a lagoon, so guests walk a circuit and return to the entrance without retracing steps. Universal Studios Florida (1990) and Islands of Adventure (1999) are the textbook cases: both place their themed areas around a central body of water, with Port of Entry acting as the single gateway. Tokyo DisneySea does something similar with its ports of call around Mediterranean Harbor.

A linear layout runs lands along a single spine. It suits narrow sites, shopping-street parks and indoor halls, and it gives designers tight control over [reveal and concealment](../story-experience/reveal-and-concealment.md) because every guest sees scenes in the same order.

The trade-off against [hub-and-spoke](../masterplanning/hub-and-spoke.md) is legibility. In a loop there is no central vantage point, so [wayfinding](../human-factors/wayfinding.md) leans on signage and landmarks, and a closed ride at the far side can leave a dead zone. On the plus side, loops spread [guest flow](../masterplanning/guest-flow-patterns.md) around the edge instead of piling it into one plaza, and the water in the middle gives every land a long view across to its neighbours.
