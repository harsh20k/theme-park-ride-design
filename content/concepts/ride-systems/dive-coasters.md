![Oblivion at Alton Towers, the first Dive Coaster, holding at the top of its drop](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d2/AltonTowers-Oblivion.JPG)
*Photo: DAllardyce, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons — [source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:AltonTowers-Oblivion.JPG)*

A dive coaster is built around one beat: the train creeps over the crest of a near-vertical drop, stops, hangs there, then falls. B&M's **holding brake** at the lip is the device that makes it work, and it turns the physics of a drop into theatre ([anticipation and dread](../human-factors/anticipation-and-dread.md), [tension and release](../story-experience/tension-and-release.md)). Oblivion at Alton Towers, opened in 1998 with an 87-degree drop into a hole in the ground, was the first; its designer John Wardley staged the whole queue around the hole.

The trains are unusual: only two or three rows, but each row seats up to ten across. That means everyone is close to the front and sees over the edge, and it keeps capacity high even though trains are short, a neat piece of [throughput math](../operations-business/throughput-math.md). Floorless versions, from Griffon (2007) onward, use a drop-away station floor so legs dangle.

Later models added a 90-degree drop and splashdown (SheiKra, 2005), vertical loops (Yukon Striker, 2019) and a beyond-vertical 96-degree first drop (Wrath of Rakshasa, 2025). The heavy wide trains need beefier track, and the drop profile gives a moment of [airtime](../ride-engineering/airtime-and-negative-g.md) at release, while the [brake types](../ride-engineering/brake-types.md) must hold a full train stationary on a slope.
