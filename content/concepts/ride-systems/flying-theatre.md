A flying theatre is a simulator with the cabin walls removed. Guests sit in rows that are lifted away from the floor and pushed forward into a concave dome screen, feet hanging, with wind, mist and scents blown at them ([wind, heat and scent effects](../show-technology/wind-heat-and-scent-effects.md)). The dome fills peripheral vision ([domes and wraparound screens](../show-technology/domes-and-wraparound-screens.md)), the seats tilt and sway with the aerial film, and the body accepts that it is gliding over a landscape. The format is tuned for [awe](../human-factors/awe.md), not thrill.

Disney's Soarin' Over California (2001) created the modern form. Imagineer Mark Sumner found the mechanism with an Erector Set model: three rows loaded at ground level, then swung up on cantilever arms, so one load floor replaced three. Dynamic Structures built it, with 87 riders per cycle facing an 80-foot dome. Brogent's i-Ride moved the idea to a six-DOF electric base and sold it worldwide as FlyOver and similar branded attractions;

Loading three rows at once is good for [throughput](../operations-business/throughput-math.md), but the lift cycle and the dome building are large and costly. [Flight of Passage](../history/flight-of-passage.md) shows how far the format stretches when the seat becomes a creature and the film becomes 3D.
