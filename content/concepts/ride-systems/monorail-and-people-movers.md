*Photo: DCLive, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons — [source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Disneyland_Mark_VII_Monorail_-_Blue.jpg)*

A monorail is a train that rides a single beam. In the **straddle** type, developed by ALWEG in the 1950s, the car wraps around the top of a concrete or steel beam on rubber tyres, with guide wheels gripping the sides; in the **suspended** type the car hangs beneath the beam, as on Wuppertal's 1901 Schwebebahn. Disneyland opened the first daily-operating monorail in the United States in 1959 as a Tomorrowland showpiece, then extended it to the hotel, turning a ride into transit.

Walt Disney World made the monorail the spine of the resort, linking car parks, hotels and two parks, so arrival itself became the [entry sequence](../masterplanning/entry-sequence.md) and the hotel-to-park journey a sales pitch ([resort integration](../masterplanning/resort-integration.md), [parking and transit](../masterplanning/parking-and-transit.md)). Elevated beams also give a moving balcony: carefully routed, a monorail offers postcard [sightlines](../story-experience/sightlines.md) over a lagoon or through a hotel lobby.

**People movers** are the smaller cousins: automated, driverless cabins on a guideway or cable, from the Ford Magic Skyway at the [1964 World's Fair](../history/worlds-fair-1964.md), ancestor of the Omnimover, to the Tomorrowland PeopleMover and Doppelmayr's rope-hauled Cable Liner shuttles at Las Vegas resorts and airports. Their job is steady, high-capacity flow with no operator per vehicle.
