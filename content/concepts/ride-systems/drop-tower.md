> **Formula** $v = \sqrt{2 g h}$
> where
> - $v$ is the speed at the bottom of a free fall
> - $g$ is gravitational acceleration, about 9.8 metres per second squared
> - $h$ is the height fallen before the brakes start, less in practice because of drag and wheel friction

A drop tower is thrill distilled to its simplest form: go up slowly, come down fast. A gondola of eight to forty seats is winched up a tower, held, and released. Because nothing is pushing the seats, riders float for a second or two of true weightlessness ([airtime and negative g](../ride-engineering/airtime-and-negative-g.md)) before the brakes bite. It needs almost no land, a staple of urban parks.

Two technologies dominate. Intamin pioneered the modern tower in the 1980s and added **magnetic braking** in the 1990s: copper fins on the gondola pass fixed permanent magnets and eddy currents slow it with no contact and no power, a brake that cannot fail off ([brake types](../ride-engineering/brake-types.md), [redundancy and fail-safe](../safety-standards/redundancy-and-fail-safe.md)). That let towers grow to the 119-metre Giant Drop at Dreamworld and the 126-metre Zumanjaro. S&S uses **pneumatics**, a cable and piston driven by compressed air, which can also fire riders upward (Space Shot) or pull them down faster than gravity (Turbo Drop).

The hold at the top is the ride's story beat, pure [anticipation and dread](../human-factors/anticipation-and-dread.md). Disney's [Tower of Terror](../history/tower-of-terror.md) wrapped a drop system in a hotel and a plot, pulling the car down with cables so every drop is scripted.
