A wooden coaster is not wood all the way down. The **track** is a stack of laminated planks, usually Southern yellow pine, with a flat **steel strip** screwed on top for the wheels to run on. Side-friction guide wheels and upstop wheels grip the stack's edges. The structure beneath is a dense lattice of timber **bents**, which is why wooden coasters look like a forest of crossed beams.

The material is why they feel the way they do. Wood flexes, so the track settles and shifts with the seasons, giving the characteristic rattle that fans love and maintenance crews track closely ([fatigue](../ride-engineering/fatigue.md), [wood vs steel](../ride-engineering/wood-vs-steel.md)). Wood also cannot be bent into tight loops, so the genre leans on drops, airtime hills and sharp laterals rather than inversions.

Modern builders have changed the recipe. Great Coasters International and The Gravity Group design twisted layouts and gentler trains, and prefabricated laser-cut track from Intamin and RMC gives wood a steel-like smoothness. The [Coney Island](../history/coney-island.md) Cyclone of 1927, still running, shows how long the formula can last, and why wood trades on [nostalgia](../human-factors/nostalgia.md) as much as thrill.
