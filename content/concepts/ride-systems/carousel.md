A carousel is a round platform turning about a centre pole, with riders on carved animals hung from poles. Its mechanism was perfected by Frederick Savage in 1880: a ring of offset cranks above the platform lifts and drops each pole as the ride turns, so the horses gallop. The first steam-driven roundabouts appeared at English fairs around 1861; electric motors and lights gave the ride its familiar look by 1900.

One quirk tells you where a machine came from. British gallopers turn clockwise, so riders mount from the left as on a real horse; American and continental carousels turn anticlockwise. Surviving antiques are treasured: the 1780 Hanau carousel still operates, and the Looff machine at Santa Cruz has run in place since 1911. Chance Rides and Bertazzon make new ones, and the figures remain a craft of [figure fabrication](../design-process/figure-fabrication.md) in wood or fibreglass.

For a park the carousel is social glue. It is the ride a toddler, a teenager and a grandparent can share ([age differences](../human-factors/age-differences.md)), it photographs beautifully at night, and placed at the hub it reads as a lantern-like [central icon](../masterplanning/central-icon.md). Its real engine is [nostalgia](../human-factors/nostalgia.md), inherited from the [pleasure gardens](../history/pleasure-gardens-and-worlds-fairs.md) where it began.
