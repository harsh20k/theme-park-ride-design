*Photo: Basilico, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons — [source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Raptor_Gardaland_2011.jpg)*

A wing coaster puts two riders on each side of the track, on seats that stick out like wings from the wheel carriage. Nobody sits over the rails. From the outside seat there is no track, no car and no floor in view, which is as close as a sit-down coaster gets to the feeling of a hang-glider. B&M opened the first, Raptor at Gardaland, in 2011; Intamin had tested an earlier "wing rider" train on Furius Baco in 2007 and sells its own wing model.

The geometry has consequences. Because riders sit far from the track centre, every roll swings the outer seats through a wide arc, so track must be designed around the rider's [heartline](../ride-engineering/heartline.md) rather than the rail, and [lateral g](../ride-engineering/lateral-g.md) is felt unevenly across a row. The same offset makes the signature trick easy: **near-miss** elements such as keyholes, cut-outs and fly-throughs that pass centimetres from riders' heads, which is a built-in [reveal and concealment](../story-experience/reveal-and-concealment.md) beat. Those stunts demand careful [reach envelope](../safety-standards/reach-envelope.md) checks.

Examples include The Swarm at Thorpe Park, GateKeeper at Cedar Point and Thunderbird at Holiday World, the first launched wing coaster.
