A mirror is the cheapest way to double a set, and a pair of mirrors is the cheapest way to make it infinite. The classic infinity mirror puts lights between a fully reflective mirror and a partially reflective one; light bounces back and forth, each reflection a little farther and a little dimmer, so a shallow box looks like a tunnel. Yayoi Kusama built whole rooms on the idea. Parks use the same trick for endless corridors, bottomless shafts and star fields, and a tilted mirror can hide the ceiling of a tiny room entirely.

Mirror mazes go back further. Gustav Castan patented a mirror labyrinth in several countries between 1888 and 1895, and amusement parks have run "houses of mirrors" ever since, often adding curved mirrors for distortion.

Mirrors are honest about one thing: they obey the viewer's own [depth cues](../human-factors/depth-cues.md), so they feel real rather than filmed. That makes them powerful in [walkthroughs](../story-experience/story-rooms-and-walkthroughs.md), for playing with [scale](../story-experience/scale-and-intimacy.md), and for the quiet [awe](../human-factors/awe.md) of a space that seems to go on forever. The cost is sightline discipline: one stray reflection of a work light breaks the spell.
