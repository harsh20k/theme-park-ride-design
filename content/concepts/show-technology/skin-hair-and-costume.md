The skin is the thinnest layer of a figure and the one guests actually judge. Builders use foam latex for a light, soft skin, silicone for the most lifelike translucency, and polyurethane as a cheaper middle option; acrylic serves for eyes and teeth. Beneath the skin sits an under-structure that spreads actuator force so a brow lifts as a soft fold rather than a dent. Hair is punched or laid strand by strand, and the costume is tailored around hose and cable exits so it hides the machine without binding a moving joint.

All of this is consumable. Skin creeps, tears at the mouth corners and yellows under show lighting; Disney's A-100 figures need new skin at least every five years, and Lincoln's 1984 rebuild came with new skin and costume developed with University of Utah scientists. That makes skin a [maintenance and lifecycle](../operations-business/maintenance-and-lifecycle.md) cost, not a one-off [fabrication](../design-process/figure-fabrication.md) item. Paint and finish borrow directly from [scenic painting](../design-process/scenic-painting.md), and getting translucency and micro-detail right is one of the few defences against the [uncanny valley](../human-factors/uncanny-valley.md).
