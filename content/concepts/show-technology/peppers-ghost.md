Pepper's ghost is a reflection trick. A large pane of glass stands at an angle, usually 45 degrees, between the audience and the stage. Off to the side or below, hidden from view, an actor or figure is brightly lit. The glass reflects that figure so faintly that it appears to stand inside the real scene behind the glass, see-through and ghostly. Dim the hidden light and the ghost fades; brighten it and the ghost solidifies.

Engineer Henry Dircks conceived it, and John Henry Pepper made it work in a theatre, first showing it at London's Royal Polytechnic in December 1862 with a scene from Dickens's The Haunted Man. The pair patented it in 1863. Disney's [Haunted Mansion](../history/haunted-mansion.md) uses it in a 90-foot ballroom scene viewed through a 30-foot pane of glass; the waltzing animatronic ghosts actually dance in black rooms above and below the guests' mezzanine, so the empty ballroom fills with transparent dancers.

Because the ghost is a true optical image, it sits at a real depth and shifts correctly as the vehicle moves, a [depth cue](../human-factors/depth-cues.md) that flat projection cannot fake. The idea remains a staple of [theatrical staging](../story-experience/theatre-staging.md) and [reveal](../story-experience/reveal-and-concealment.md) design.
