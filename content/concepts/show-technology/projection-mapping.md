Projection mapping means bending a picture so it fits an object instead of a flat screen. The content is built on a model of the surface, warped from the projector's exact position and lens, then masked so light only falls where it should. Nothing about the trick is new to parks: when the Haunted Mansion opened in 1969, singers' faces shot on 16 mm film were projected onto sculpted busts in the graveyard, and the same idea gives Madame Leota her talking head. In 2015 the Hatbox Ghost returned with its face projected onto the figure, fixing an illusion that ambient light had defeated in 1969.

At the other end of the scale, nightly castle shows at Magic Kingdom and other parks map video onto the whole [central icon](../masterplanning/central-icon.md), turning the day-time landmark into a [night-time](../sensory-environment/night-vs-day.md) screen. Large shows stack or [blend](../show-technology/projector-blending.md) many projectors for brightness.

The craft lives at the seam between media and architecture: the [themed facade](../design-process/themed-facades.md) must be light-coloured and matte enough to take the image, and any gap between projection and surface, even a centimetre, breaks the magic.
