> **Formula** $\Delta L = \alpha\,L\,\Delta T$
> where:
> - $\Delta L$ — how much a member grows or shrinks
> - $\alpha$ — the linear expansion coefficient; for steel roughly 11 to 13 millionths per kelvin
> - $L$ — the member's original length
> - $\Delta T$ — the temperature change it experiences

Everything on a ride is slightly longer at four in the afternoon than at dawn. Steel grows by about twelve parts in a million per degree of warming, negligible until multiplied by a long structure. A hundred metres of continuous track taken through a forty-degree swing between a winter night and a summer afternoon changes length by around 48 millimetres. Lock that movement in and the force becomes enormous; railways call the result a sun kink.

Coaster track is therefore never one rigid piece. It is bolted up from sections with joints that allow a little movement, columns may lean fractionally, and long straight launch and brake runs get the most attention. Dark steel in full sun runs far hotter than the air, so surface temperature is what matters; shaded or indoor rides see smaller swings, a quiet advantage of [indoor parks](../masterplanning/indoor-parks.md) and of [shade and microclimate](../sensory-environment/shade-and-microclimate.md) planning. Wood moves too, but mainly with moisture.

Temperature also shows in the data: sensor positions, brake gaps and wheel compounds all shift with it, which is why [testing and commissioning](../design-process/testing-and-commissioning.md) runs repeat across seasons and joint gaps are routine in [inspections](../safety-standards/inspections.md). How each [track type](../ride-engineering/track-types.md) is jointed decides how gracefully it breathes.
