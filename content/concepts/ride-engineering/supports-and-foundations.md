The first sign of a new coaster is a field of holes. Each becomes a footer: a reinforced concrete block, sometimes a pile cap on piles driven to firmer ground, with anchor bolts cast in to a surveyed pattern. A steel column lands on a base plate, is levelled on grout, and bolted down. Coaster101 traces the forces through that joint: compression through plate and grout, shear through a pin and bolts, tension through the bolts alone.

Tension is the interesting case. In a valley the train pushes the track down, but over an airtime hill the upstop wheels pull the track, and so the column, upward. Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension, which is why reinforcement and anchor bolts matter. Whether a site needs spread footings or deep piles depends on the soil, making ground conditions part of [site selection](../masterplanning/site-selection.md).

Above ground, tall steel rides are slender and sway; wooden structures move visibly under a train. Coasters in hurricane country are designed to fall sideways if the worst happens. Footers must also respect what comes next, tying into [expansion and phasing](../masterplanning/expansion-and-phasing.md), the [rockwork](../design-process/rockwork.md) that hides columns, and the sequencing of [site construction](../design-process/site-construction.md).
