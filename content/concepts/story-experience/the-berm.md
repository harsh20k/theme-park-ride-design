The berm is a long mound of earth, usually planted with trees, that wraps around a theme park. Walt Disney wanted Disneyland ringed by a railroad on a raised embankment so that, once inside, guests could not see Anaheim. Disneyland historians put the mound at roughly ten feet; that figure comes from a single secondary source and is not confirmed by Disney, so treat it as approximate.

The berm does three jobs at once. It hides the outside world and the park's own backstage, so the only things in view belong to the story. It gives the park train a scenic right of way. And it draws a hard edge: crossing under it, through a tunnel beneath the tracks, is the moment the story starts. Later parks use the same idea with walls, buildings and landscaping where there is no room for earth. At Disney's Hollywood Studios an earthen berm with a tunnel separates Galaxy's Edge from the rest of the park.

It is a tool of [onstage/backstage](../masterplanning/onstage-backstage.md) planning and [landscape architecture](../masterplanning/landscape-architecture.md), a reason [park trains](../ride-systems/park-trains.md) ride high, and a quiet guardian of [suspension of disbelief](../human-factors/suspension-of-disbelief.md).
