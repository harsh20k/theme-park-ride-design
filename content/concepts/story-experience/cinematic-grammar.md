Film has a grammar: a wide establishing shot tells you where you are, a medium shot shows who is there, a close-up shows what matters, and a cut moves you between them. Disneyland's first designers were film art directors, so they composed the park the same way. The Main Street train station is an establishing shot. The street is a slow tracking move toward the castle. A shop window with one lit prop is a close-up.

In a park the guest holds the camera, so the designer controls the shot by controlling where they can stand and look. A gap in a wall frames a view; a turn in the path is a cut; a long straight approach is a slow push-in. John Hench described park sequences in exactly these terms, and film textbooks such as Bordwell and Thompson's Film Art supply the vocabulary of mise-en-scene and continuity that show writers reuse.

The grammar runs through [previs](../design-process/previs.md), where a ride is storyboarded like a film, through [colour scripts](../sensory-environment/color-scripts.md), and literally through [media dark rides](../ride-systems/media-dark-ride.md), which are films with the audience in the moving camera. It is also applied [visual attention](../human-factors/visual-attention.md): whatever is framed is what gets seen.
