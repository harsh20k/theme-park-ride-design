A sightline is the straight line from a guest's eye to whatever is in front of it. Visual intrusion is anything on that line that does not belong to the story: a plain show building, a crane, a car park, another land's mountain. Themed design is a long argument with sightlines, because the box that holds the ride is usually bigger and uglier than the facade that pretends to be it.

The tools are simple. Put the box behind a [berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md) or partly underground, as Disneyland's Haunted Mansion does with its show building behind the railroad. Paint exposed surfaces in a dull [go-away green](../sensory-environment/go-away-green.md). Use [planting](../sensory-environment/planting-design.md) and facades to close the gaps. Keep the tallest element in each land inside the story, which is one reason Expedition Everest's mountain, not a service building, is the thing you see from the path.

Sightline studies are part of [onstage/backstage](../masterplanning/onstage-backstage.md) planning and become critical in [expansion and phasing](../masterplanning/expansion-and-phasing.md), when a new land's construction can appear over an old one's rooftops. Doug Chiang notes that a park needs more completeness than a film because a guest can walk through any unexpected door.
