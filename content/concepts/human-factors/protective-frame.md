Apter describes three nested zones. In the safety zone nothing threatens you. In the danger zone you can sense harm but a barrier holds it off. Beyond the dangerous edge is trauma, where harm actually happens. A protective frame is whatever convinces you the barrier will hold, and he lists three kinds: confidence in your own skill, a safety zone such as a restraint or a railing, and detachment, the observer's frame you have while watching a film.

Rides stack all three. Guests borrow confidence from the engineering, sit inside a safety zone built from [restraints](../human-factors/restraint-fit.md) and visible [inspections](../safety-standards/inspections.md), and gain detachment from story: "it is only a haunted house". That is why [suspension of disbelief](../human-factors/suspension-of-disbelief.md) and trust reinforce each other.

The frame is fragile. A loose lap bar, a sudden stop with no announcement, or a rusted support in view can snap it, and the same ride becomes frightening. Managing [risk perception](../human-factors/risk-perception-and-trust.md) is therefore part of the show, not just of [safety compliance](../safety-standards/risk-assessment.md).
