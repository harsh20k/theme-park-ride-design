Stand at a cliff edge with no railing and you feel dread. Add a railing and you lean out to enjoy the view. The drop did not change; your sense of protection did. Apter calls the railing a protective frame, and argues that excitement lives right at "the dangerous edge": close enough to feel the risk, held back from the harm.

Theme parks are machines for building railings. A [drop tower](../ride-systems/drop-tower.md) lets you fall from a height that would kill you, inside a [restraint](../ride-engineering/restraint-mechanisms.md) you trust. The Twilight Zone Tower of Terror sells a broken, plunging elevator, a nightmare in a real hotel, inside a story and a ride system guests know is engineered. Andrade and Cohen found that horror fans actually feel fear *and* pleasure at once, as long as the frame holds.

The design lesson: every cue of safety, from clean stations to confident [operators](../safety-standards/operator-training.md), raises how much danger guests will enjoy.
