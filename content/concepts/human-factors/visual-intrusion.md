Visual intrusion is the planner's name for a contradiction you can see: a modern office block over the castle wall, a floodlight mast behind a Wild West street, a coaster from another land looming over a quiet forest scene. Walt Disney's answer at Disneyland was the [berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md), an earth wall that turns the park's boundary into a Lynch-style edge and hides everything beyond it. When land outside the berm was built on, the problem returned, one reason Disney bought so much of central Florida.

The method is sightline analysis. From every point a guest can stand, designers draw the cone of view and check what falls in it, the subject of the [sightlines](../story-experience/sightlines.md) card. Fixes come in layers: move the offending object, block it with trees or a facade, lift the guest's eye with a weenie, or paint it to vanish with [go-away green](../sensory-environment/go-away-green.md). The hardest cases come from the park's own growth: [resort hotels](../masterplanning/resort-integration.md) that want a view of the castle give the castle a view of the hotel, and [expansion phasing](../masterplanning/expansion-and-phasing.md) means cranes stand in sightlines for years.

One out-of-place object is enough to tell a guest's brain that the whole place is a set.
