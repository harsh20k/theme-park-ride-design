In *The Image of the City* (1960), Kevin Lynch asked residents of Boston, Jersey City and Los Angeles to draw their cities from memory. Five elements kept appearing: **paths** people move along, **edges** that bound an area, **districts** with a shared character, **nodes** where paths meet and decisions are made, and **landmarks** used as reference points.

A theme park is almost a textbook Lynch diagram. Main Street is a path. The [berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md) is an edge that hides the outside world. Each themed land is a district with its own music, paving and colour. The hub is the master node, and the castle or [central icon](../masterplanning/central-icon.md) is the landmark visible from most of it. [Land transitions](../masterplanning/land-transitions.md) are where edges and districts are deliberately blurred so you cross without noticing.

Designers can audit a plan with these five words. A dead-end path without a landmark at its end, or a node with six exits and no visual cue, predicts where guests will stop and stare at their phones. Lynch's categories also explain why big [weenies](../story-experience/weenies.md) work: a landmark seen from many nodes stitches the whole mental map together.
