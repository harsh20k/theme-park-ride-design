Elaine Hatfield and colleagues described emotional contagion as a three-step reflex. We automatically mimic the faces, voices and postures around us; the feedback from our own body then nudges our feelings toward theirs; the result is emotional convergence in a group, usually within seconds and without anyone deciding to feel anything.

On a ride this is a free amplifier. One screamer at the crest of the lift makes the row behind scream too, and each scream raises the arousal of everyone who hears it. That is a reason rows face the same way, why open trains feel wilder than enclosed cars, and why [seat layout](../ride-engineering/seat-and-containment.md) is partly a social decision. A nearly empty train is measurably less fun.

Contagion runs through staff too. Performers in [cast role-play](../story-experience/cast-role-play.md) seed a mood that guests pick up, and a crowd at a [parade](../masterplanning/parade-and-show-crowds.md) synchronises on the loudest faces. The downside: a panicked or bored guest spreads just as fast, so [group dynamics](../human-factors/group-dynamics.md) in queues deserve attention.
