Speed has no sensor of its own. The inner ear reports acceleration, not steady velocity, so once a train stops speeding up the body goes quiet. What remains is the eye, and specifically the edge of the eye. Brandt, Dichgans and Koenig showed that peripheral vision dominates the sense of self-motion: a scene streaming past at the edges says "moving" far more loudly than the same motion viewed straight ahead.

Optic flow is faster for nearer objects. A coaster at 60 km/h through an open field feels modest; the same train through a tunnel, under a headchopper beam or between tight [track elements](../ride-engineering/track-elements.md) feels furious. Designers therefore spend near-objects deliberately: trees from [planting design](../sensory-environment/planting-design.md) beside a low turn, a trench cut through the [berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md), supports threaded close to the line. Space Mountain is the extreme case, a coaster of ordinary speed made to feel fast by darkness that removes distance cues and leaves only flashing near-field lights.

The same principle in reverse calms a ride: a [speed profile](../ride-engineering/speed-profile.md) that keeps the fastest sections high and open will read gentler than its numbers.
