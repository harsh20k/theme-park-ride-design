Reviewers at design studios all say it: show how you think. Falcon's Creative Group staff advise showing your personality and reasoning, not only what you can produce, and studying the portfolios of studios you admire to calibrate level and style. Architecture and engineering candidates should show both creative vision and technical rigour; one engineer suggests personal builds that reveal how you solve problems.

Structure each project as a short story. Open with the hook and the brief, then show the process: sketches, plans, a [model or maquette](../design-process/models-and-maquettes.md), a [previs](../design-process/previs.md) pass, the options you rejected. Close with the result and your exact role on it. This mirrors how studios document [design intent](../design-process/design-intent.md), so it signals you already work their way.

Pick projects that prove depth in your lane. For a concept artist that is [concept art](../design-process/concept-art.md) that reads as a place, not a mood. For an engineer, a competition build with the calculations attached. Don Carson's pre-Imagineering portfolio was about 150 paintings of Disneyland details, which showed he understood the parks as designed objects. Former Imagineer Brian Collins warns students that copying everyone else's portfolio makes you indistinguishable. Entries from [competitions](../careers/competitions.md) such as Imaginations are ready-made portfolio chapters.
