Every park is full of things guests should not notice: electrical cabinets, show-building walls, speaker poles, fire doors, the fence that keeps the parking lot out of a Victorian street. Rather than hide all of them behind scenery, designers paint them in a small family of dull, mid-value greens and greys that the eye slides past. Disney's version is nicknamed "Go-Away Green", and its grey cousin is sometimes called "No-See-Um Grey"; neither is an official paint code so much as a practice. The trick works because of how [light and colour](../sensory-environment/light-and-color.md) compete for attention. A low-saturation green matches foliage and shadow, and it carries none of the contrast that makes themed facades pop, so attention goes to the facade instead. The same palette helps [the berm](../story-experience/the-berm.md) finish the job of sealing the world, and it quietly supports [forced perspective](../story-experience/forced-perspective.md) by keeping the real edges of a building from reading as edges.
